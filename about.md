Welcome! I'm Tessa. I'm a PhD student at Stanford University under their Program in Modern Thought and Literature, where I split my time between the Departments of [English](https://english.stanford.edu/) and [Political Science](https://politicalscience.stanford.edu/). But you can usually find me at [CESTA](https://cesta.stanford.edu/), Stanford's Digital Humanities hub. 

My research asks both literary scholars and computer scientists how we should understand large language models (LLMs) as a [*cultural technology*](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adt9819): as a technology that takes in and spits out information, and influences a discourse we might call "culture" in the process. 
- How should we interpret LLM-generated text – not for what it says about the model that produced it, or "me" (the user) that prompts it, but instead for what it says about *us*, the culture that creates it? 
- How should we evaluate and build AI models to approach standards of plural and collective life, not just to meet benchmarks that collapse interpretive diversity?

My work draws on the sub-fields of literary history, of the digital humanities, and of science and technology studies. In its social and political framing, my work is indebted to the thought and legacy of Hannah Arendt. 

My most recent projects, spanning the literary and political sides of cultural AI research, are: in collaboration with [Nina Beguš](https://www.ninabegus.com/) and [Gašper Beguš](https://www.gasperbegus.com/), [two neural networks](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7155038) trained on James Joyce's *Finnegans Wake*, the text that reimagined global language technologies decades before LLMs; and an [ICML workshop paper](https://openreview.net/forum?id=uBTzTVTEBo) on agonistic approaches to AI design in the public sphere.

Questions I'm interested in at the moment are:
1. How should we contextualize LLMs within literary history? How do we know what we're reading when we read LLM-generated text, and how have people thought about this in earlier periods of technological innovation?
2. What would it look like not just for us to read LLM-generated text, but also for LLMs to critically engage with our culturally motive texts, and how would (or should) we evaluate them?
3. What do calls for ["pluralistic alignment"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05070) from within the ML community get wrong about the problem they're trying to solve, whether technically, theoretically, culturally, empirically, or all of the above? What infrastructure should we provide instead – as educators, as developers, and as community members?

I received my MPhil in Modern Languages from the University of Oxford as a [Rhodes Scholar](https://www.rhodeshouse.ox.ac.uk), and my AB in Chemistry and Comparative Literature from Harvard College. I live in Palo Alto, CA, during the academic terms and London, UK, otherwise, and I’m always happy to connect with researchers working on similar topics in either (or both) locales. 

Prior to graduate work in the humanities, I worked in computational biology and biochemistry research under Drs. [Alex Shalek](https://shaleklab.com), [George Whitesides](https://www.gmwgroup.harvard.edu), and [Nicholas Polizzi](https://www.polizzilab.org/) at Harvard and MIT. I still give guest lectures on scientific paper-writing for Harvard's undergraduate courses in [experimental chemistry](https://my.harvard.edu/course/CHEM165/2027-Spring/LAB).
